# Good design and generic tells

The single home for what makes work read as generated and how to avoid it. Read it when creating a direction, testing a plan for genericity, or when a result looks templated, in any medium.

Generated work tends toward the average of everything the model has seen: nothing in it is wrong and nothing in it was decided. The cure is decisions taken from the subject, in this order: the idea, then substance, and only then the tells. Removing tells from a piece with no idea produces an empty piece with good manners.

## 1. Inventory the subject

Before any styling, list what belongs to this client and to no competitor, aiming for eight to twelve items. A short brief gives fewer: work with those and ask only for what would change the idea.

- its name and what the name means; its address, street and neighbourhood, and how that place looks (tiles, signs, paint, shop-front lettering, light, weather);
- the numbers in the brief (seats, rooms, minutes, price, year);
- the constraint it lives with (one batch a month, closed in winter, by appointment);
- its materials and colours; its rituals and hours (the oven lit at five, the ferry at seven);
- the printed things of its world (timetable, label, ticket, sign).

Use only what is true: an item you have not confirmed is a question, not a foundation. This list feeds the idea below and doubles as the search list for [imagery](imagery.md). For software, inventory the objects, real magnitudes and jobs instead; see [product UI](product-ui.md).

## 2. Write the idea

A style is not an idea. Do not start building until both blanks come from the subject:

> In this piece, **[something that belongs to the subject]** becomes **[the device that carries it]**.

Places to look for the first blank: what it produces, its object or silhouette, its name, its place, its ritual, the artefact of its world, or the opposite category that owns a feeling it lacks.

**Competitor test:** put the nearest real competitor in the sentence. If it still works, the idea belongs to the category: a loaf is every bakery; the seven doors of the street this one is named after are only this one. "Clean and modern" never passes.

**Guess test (the look):** hold the proposal against its category. If someone could guess this aesthetic from the subject alone, or from the subject plus its obvious antidote, neither answer is a decision. The antidote is not the way out; §5 records what happens when everyone takes it.

**Carry it through.** The commonest failure, even in good work, is a distinctive opening followed by stock blocks. The idea must reach the pricing, the FAQ, the footer, the last scene.

**One live thing, when it fits.** On interactive pieces, consider one thing the visitor can do or that differs per visit, made from the idea and its real content: pick a wood and see the chair in it, set the loan and read the real monthly figure, see whether the hut's stove is lit. It works by thumb, has a plain fallback and never blocks reading. It is an option, not a requirement; do not add one where it would be a toy.

## 3. Substance

Restraint without content reads as unfinished. In the blind tests behind this guidance, a richer piece with template devices beat a cleaner, thinner one, and a page so quiet it read as empty was punished.

- Fill space with scale, full content, a picture made for this subject or a committed field of colour, never with another ornament.
- Deliver every section the brief asks for, completely and at a readable size: the whole menu with prices, each room with its details, every speaker.
- Design at real density. Sample details (dish names, room names and rates) are fine where the brief leaves gaps, declared as samples and listed for confirmation before launch. Proof is different: customers, quotes, reviews, press, logos and performance or business claims are never invented. Leave a visible neutral marker such as "—" where such a figure is missing.
- Words: on a conversion page they belong to `landing-launch`, whose copywriting reference owns the "sounds like AI" patterns. Elsewhere, apply the swap test: could another company print this sentence unchanged?

## 4. Placed space or hole

Empty space is a rest only when something closes it: the image with text on its edge, a picture on the far side, a headline spanning the gap, content in the corners. If nothing closes it, it is a hole. When a section looks empty the answer is rarely to add something: enlarge the main element or cut the section.

Common origins, most frequent first: section gaps that are too large; text in one half and nothing in the other; content that arrives late (a reveal that did not fire); a short block stretched to a full screen; a nearly empty pinned scene; pictures of different heights aligned at the top.

## 5. The tells (registry dated November 2026)

A tell is a device produced by default, without a reason. It is a prompt to justify the choice, not an automatic failure: the brand or the brief can make any of these right, and a brand system always wins. Mostly proven patterns with a distinctive minority is a healthy mix.

Tells expire. Every published "use this instead" list has become the next wave, so replacements here are procedures (derive from the subject, choose a [composition family](composition-families.md)), never a named font, colour or component. Re-date this registry when you revise it.

**First wave (2023-2024).** Violet or indigo gradients on hero, buttons and glows; gradient-filled headlines; glass panels as a substitute for concept; decorative gradient blobs; effect-driven 3D; glowing buttons; a soft grey shadow under every card; three identical cards with an icon in a rounded square; emoji as icons or bullets; centred headline, subtitle, two buttons and a screenshot below; the same default typeface on every page.

**Second wave (2025).** A tiny tracked-caps eyebrow above every heading (more than one per three sections needs a reason); a pill badge above the hero; one headline word styled differently; bento grids of rounded tiles; near-black with one neon or acid accent and a glow; floating blurred pill navigation; decorative status dots ("Available for work"); fade-and-rise on every section.

**Third wave (2025-2026), the remedy that became the look.** "Warm it up and make it editorial": cream ground with terracotta or rust; high-contrast serif as a shortcut to "premium"; oversized italic serif headlines; broadsheet cosplay on subjects that are not news; performative labels ("Field notes", "Quietly trusted by"). Warmth can come instead from a low-chroma stone, a deep ink, a photograph or one full warm field.

**Gallery costume.** Monospace micro-labels as a "technical" voice, 01/02/03 index numbering, ruled grids, design-tool chrome as ornament (crosshairs, crop marks, handles), agency micro-meta (local time, coordinates, version stamps, "Scroll"), arrows on every link, tiny tracked captions scattered around the viewport. These are common on award sites, and generators copy them first. A brand can own them, and then they stay, used where they carry meaning; without brand or meaning they are costume that only signals "designed". Mono labels and numbering are more common on the study's top sites (table below), so the suspicion here is judgement about defaults, not a measured defect.

**Surfaces and type.** A coloured stripe down one edge of a card, callout or alert; a hard offset block shadow outside a world that is actually neobrutalist; muted text in plain grey on a coloured field instead of a tint of that hue; a grid, stripe or dot texture standing in for a ground; an illustration assembled from rounded rectangles and circles where a drawing of the subject belongs; a geometric mask or radial cutout tracing a photographed subject's edge instead of a real cut-out. The faces models reach for by default deserve the same suspicion, and naming a default is not the same as prescribing a replacement: Inter, Roboto, Fraunces, Geist, Playfair Display, Space Grotesk, Plus Jakarta Sans, DM Sans, Instrument Sans. Using one costs a reason no other face could satisfy; a subject association ("books want a serif") is not that reason.

**Structure.**
- A full sentence set at display size over three or four lines.
- The stock order of hero, logos, features, testimonial, pricing, CTA, instead of the order of the argument.
- Every section the same layout family; image and text zigzag three times; more than three equal columns in a row.
- Everything centred; one medium radius on everything; nine nearly equal font sizes.
- A hero with more than about four text elements.
- Invented social proof; a CSS box or grey-bar "screenshot" where a real capture or an honest placeholder belongs.
- Designer controls (viewport toggles, tweak panels) left in the final product.

**Motion.**
- The same ease, the same 0.4 s and the same offset-and-fade on everything, with an ambient zoom on every scene.
- A dozen staggered entrances in the hero; hover lift on every card; bounce or elastic easing on interface elements.
- Loops that mean nothing: pulsing dots, floating shapes, shimmering text.
- Scrambled or typewriter headlines that stay unreadable for seconds; content at zero opacity for more than about 900 ms; motion crossing text being read.
- A reveal that never fires and leaves a blank band.

The remedies live in [motion graphics](motion-graphics.md).

**Dashboards and app screens** have their own list in [product UI](product-ui.md).

## 6. What the evidence supports

The table comes from a 2026 study of about 247 One Page Love and Awwwards sites, scored by model reviewers from screenshots; other figures in these references also draw on a later study of about 100 Awwwards Sites of the Day. Their test rounds carried about half a point of noise. Both measure gallery fashion as much as quality, associations are not causes, and the skill built on them never beat a no-skill baseline on average score. Use the figures to calibrate suspicion, not as rules. A small tracked-caps mono label falls in two rows with opposite signals: the data points at tiny tracked text, not at the typeface.

| Device | Sites scored 7.5+ | Sites under 6.5 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Icon-topped feature cards | 1% | 28% |
| Tiny tracked-caps labels, any | 16% | 57% |
| Pill badges | 14% | 34% |
| Gradient fills | 22% | 52% |
| Gradient text | 2% | 13% |
| Shadows on resting elements | 24% | 48% |
| Emoji | 10% | 26% |
| Backdrop blur (no real difference) | 32% | 39% |
| Italic-serif accent word (no real difference) | 4% | 7% |
| Thin rules (no real difference) | 61% | 52% |
| Monospace labels (more common on top sites) | 44% | 24% |
| Index numbering (more common on top sites) | 38% | 21% |

Model jurors rewarded, in the order they named it: an idea that belongs to this client and not the category; one live thing; full content at a readable size; a first screen with one dominant element and a real action; drawings of the subject with detail. They punished a page so quiet it read as empty, small headlines, text sliding over pictures, recolouring mid-section, rows cut at the right edge, and navigation that took over the first screen.

The real test is still a person looking at the real render against the brief; see [quality bar](quality-bar.md).
